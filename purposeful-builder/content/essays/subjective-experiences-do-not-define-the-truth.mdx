In dealing with young people, one thing I have come to observe is that many have different experiences of what fatherhood means. Some have fathers who love them deeply, while others have experienced hostility, rejection or emotional absence. Some receive love in abundance, while others struggle to receive even the basic expression of it.

Our experiences are often shaped by the circumstances surrounding our birth, our position in the family and the dispositions of our fathers.

For instance, in some parts of Eastern Nigeria, when a boy is the only male child in a family, he may receive special treatment because he is seen as the one who will carry on his father's name and legacy. Unfortunately, this special treatment can sometimes deprive him of the discipline and training he needs to become a responsible man.

There is also a way of loving a child that can destroy that child. When a father gives in to every demand, even when the child is not mature enough or qualified to handle what he is asking for, he may be doing more harm than good. Love without discipline can become indulgence, and indulgence can produce a child who is ill-prepared for life.

On the other hand, some fathers are harsh, emotionally unavailable or excessively demanding. Their children grow up with a different understanding of what it means to be loved by a father.

These experiences, whether good or bad, become the templates through which many children understand fatherhood. But here is where we must be careful: **our subjective experiences with earthly fathers must not become the standard by which we define the fatherhood of God.**

Even the best earthly fathers do not perfectly represent God's fatherhood.

### The Standard of True Fatherhood

The Bible makes an important distinction in Hebrews 12:9–10. It explains that our earthly fathers disciplined us as they thought best, but God disciplines us for our good, so that we may share in His holiness.

Notice the distinction. Our earthly fathers often discipline us according to their own understanding, preferences and sometimes even their personal interests. God, however, disciplines us with our ultimate good in view.

This is important because a father can genuinely love his child and still make decisions that are more reflective of his own desires than God's purpose for that child. Sometimes, what a father wants for his child may conflict with what God wants. We have seen situations where children make decisions that are consistent with God's direction for their lives, but their earthly fathers oppose those decisions because they are not aligned with God's will themselves.

This is why we must be careful not to impose our personal ambitions, preferences and cultural expectations on our children in the name of training them.

Proverbs 22:6 tells us to train up a child in the way he should go. That training must be informed by God's design and purpose for the child, not merely by what we want the child to become.

This does not mean that culture has no place in raising children. Our values and traditions can be passed on, but they must remain subject to God's Word. We cannot insist that a child follows a particular tradition simply because that is how things have always been done in our village or family. Whatever we teach must be consistent with God's truth.

Deuteronomy 6 gives us a beautiful picture of this responsibility. God instructed the Israelites to keep His words in their hearts and teach them diligently to their children. They were to speak about them at home, along the way, when they lay down and when they rose. His words were to be so central to their lives that they would become part of the everyday environment in which their children were raised.

There is something important here: before we teach our children God's ways, we must first allow those ways to shape us. We cannot effectively teach what we are unwilling to practise.

We are stewards of the children God has entrusted to us. And stewardship requires us to understand the heart of the One who gave us those children. We must learn from Him and allow His nature to influence how we love, correct, guide and relate with them.

### When Earthly Models Distort Divine Reality

Consider, for instance, the father who is physically present but emotionally absent. He provides school fees, buys school materials, pays bills and ensures that the basic needs of his children are met. He is also quick to correct them whenever they go wrong. But beyond providing and correcting, he rarely creates room for meaningful communication, affection or companionship.

His children may grow up knowing that he provides for them, but they may struggle to understand that he also wants to know them, listen to them and enjoy a relationship with them.

What happens when these children begin to relate with God?

They may unconsciously transfer that same understanding to Him. They approach God mainly when they have a need. They pray because they need something, seek Him because they are in trouble and call on Him when they require intervention. They may never fully understand that God desires a relationship with them beyond the things He can provide.

And when they eventually outgrow the needs that brought them to Him, they may also lose the motivation to communicate with Him.

This is one of the dangers of conditional love. When children experience a relationship built mainly around what a father provides, they may struggle to sustain genuine communication when those provisions are no longer necessary. They may carry that same transactional understanding into their relationship with God.

But God does not merely want to give us things. He wants us to know Him.

Jesus, in John 17:3, identifies eternal life as knowing the only true God and Jesus Christ whom He has sent. The essence of eternal life is not simply receiving what God can give; it is coming into a relationship with Him.

We see a distinction between knowing God's acts and knowing His ways in Psalm 103:7. The children of Israel knew His acts, but Moses knew His ways.

They saw what God could do. Moses had a deeper understanding of who God was. What God could do flowed from who He is, but many of the Israelites related with Him mainly through His acts. They were afraid to draw near, even when God was inviting them into a deeper encounter with Him.

Sometimes, our children grow up with a similar understanding of God because of the way we have modelled fatherhood to them. They know that God can provide, protect, heal and answer prayers, but they struggle to understand His love, His heart and His desire for fellowship with them.

And as fathers, we must recognise that our actions can make it easier or more difficult for our children to understand and relate with God. We will give an account to Him for how we treat the children He has entrusted to us.

### Love and Discipline: The Balance of God's Heart

The same principle applies to fathers who go to the other extreme by giving their children everything they ask for. God is love, but He is also a disciplined Father. He does not indulge us into destruction. Sometimes, out of love, He withholds what we demand because He knows we are not ready for it or that it will not be good for us. At other times, He corrects and chastises us because He is concerned about what we are becoming.

Fathers must learn to do the same. We should not be afraid to express affection, but neither should we be afraid to correct. Love must be the motivation behind both.

When love is genuinely the motivation, our focus shifts from merely satisfying a child's immediate desires to considering that child's greater good. We may sometimes have to say no, even when the child does not understand or appreciate it. We may have to correct, even when it is uncomfortable. But our actions must always be guided by the desire to help the child become who God has designed him or her to be.

This is why fatherhood is not a small responsibility. It is a weighty assignment that requires us to keep learning from God.

We must pay attention to how He fathers us. We must become students of His Word so that, with His help, we can model His heart to our children.

I have seen the difference in the lives of children whose fathers have given them a healthy understanding of love, discipline and acceptance. They may not have perfect fathers, but they have a foundation that makes it easier to understand God's love.

On the other hand, some children spend a significant part of their Christian journey trying to reprogramme their minds to accept that God genuinely loves them. They struggle to believe that He does not disqualify them because of their mistakes, that His correction does not mean rejection and that His instructions are for their good.

They have to unlearn the unhealthy picture of fatherhood they grew up with before they can fully embrace the fatherhood of God.

We should not make that journey unnecessarily difficult for our children.

Our responsibility is not to make our children see God through the limitations of our own experiences. It is to give them a glimpse of His heart through the way we live, love, discipline and relate with them.

Subjective experiences do not define the truth. Our experiences of earthly fatherhood may influence how we perceive God, but they do not determine who He is.

Let us learn His heart and model fatherhood accordingly. Our children deserve to grow up with an understanding of love that does not indulge them, discipline that does not destroy them, and a relationship that does not make them feel they must earn their father's affection.

That is the kind of foundation that can help them walk into a relationship with God, not merely knowing what He can do, but growing to know who He is.
